Research done by undergraduates

HEATlab is an undergraduate lab. Students design the algorithms, build the robot experiments, run the studies, and write the papers, often as first authors at top AI venues like AAAI and ICAPS.

Since 2014, 48 students from 5 colleges have worked in the lab, and most of them have published. See where they are now.

Research

Our mission is to create techniques for human-robot teaming: the flexible coordination of complex, interrelated activities in shared spaces, so that humans and agents together accomplish what neither could alone.

Human-robot teamwork

How should robots and AI agents share work with people? We build scheduling approaches for mixed human-robot teams and test them with real people and real robots, measuring trust, fluency, and who should take the lead.

Past projects

CHAI: Cultivating Human-Agent Interactions

As collaborative robots become commonplace, creating schedules for human-robot teamwork will be crucial. This project tested scheduling algorithms developed for robot teams on human-robot teams, using Rethink Robotics' Sawyer to study how people and robots complete tasks together, and how human autonomy affects a team's productivity.

Students: Brenda Castro, Montana Roberts

  • Who takes the lead? Automated scheduling for human-robot teams (2017)
Human-Robot Trust

How much a person trusts a robot shapes how they interact with it. Using a coin entrustment game (a variant of the prisoner's dilemma) and our NAO robot, we measured trust and cooperation as separate phenomena, comparing how people treat robots versus other humans.

Students: Jane Wu, Erin Paeng, Kari Linder

  • Trust and Cooperation in Human-Robot Decision Making (2016)
  • Exploring human-robot trust and cooperation (2016)
  • Human-Robot Trust and Cooperation Through a Game Theoretic Framework (student abstract) (2016)
MAD about Fluency

What makes a human-robot collaboration feel fluent? We built MAD-TN, a tool for measuring fluency, and used it to study human temporal uncertainty and the fluency of human-robot interactions.

Students: Seth Isaacson, Gretchen Rice, Maya Abo Dominguez, William La, Emily Weiss, Zeneve Jacotin, Amy Yuan

  • MAD-TN: A Tool for Measuring Fluency in Human-Robot Collaboration (2019)
  • Modeling Human Temporal Uncertainty in Human-Agent Teams (2020)
  • Analyzing the Fluency of Human-Robot Interactions (2023)
Improvisational Teamwork

Simple temporal networks that let people and agents coordinate on the fly, plus analysis of how dynamic control holds up when uncertainty is skewed.

Students: Malia Morgan, Julianna Schalkwyk, Yuki Wang, Hannah Davalos, Ryan Martinez, Vibha Rohilla, Rosy Chen, Eryn Ma, Ingrid Wu

  • Simple Temporal Networks for Improvisational Teamwork (2022)
  • Simple Temporal Networks for Improvisational Teamwork (2022)
  • Sensitivity Analysis for Dynamic Control of PSTNs with Skewed Distributions (2023)
PaWPal: Productivity and Wellness Pal

College students often struggle to balance work with wellness. PaWPal, an Android app, used experience sampling to model when users are most likely to experience flow and to nudge them toward more productive, immersive work. It was the precursor to the campus-wide Workload and Health at Mudd (WHAM!) study.

Students: Priya Donti, Honey Rosenbloom, Alex Gruver

  • Predicting the quality of user experiences to improve productivity and wellness (2015)
  • Exploring active and passive team-based coordination (2014)

Plans that survive the real world

Robots slip and people run late. We develop the math and algorithms behind temporal plans that stay flexible, durable, and controllable when things don't go as expected.

Past projects

Probably-in-Control

Sometimes scheduling uncertainty outstrips an agent's ability to control for it. We developed analytical tools for the “degree of controllability”: the likelihood that an agent, such as a robot, can still control for uncertainty caused by slippage or localization error.

Students: Shyan Akmal, Savana Ammons, Maggie Li, Michael Gao, Lindsay Popowski

  • Quantifying degrees of controllability in temporal networks with uncertainty (2019)
  • Quantifying controllability in temporal networks with uncertainty (2020)
Ctrl-Alt-Repeat

Dynamic control strategies for probabilistic simple temporal networks: deciding when to act as uncertain events unfold.

Students: Michael Gao, Lindsay Popowski

  • Dynamic Control of Probabilistic Simple Temporal Networks (2020)
PolyBots & Acrobotics

The constraints in a temporal plan define a polytope bounding the space of possible schedules, and its geometry says something about the plan. PolyBots used geometric properties to quantify a plan's flexibility; Acrobotics introduced “durability,” a measure of a schedule's resilience to disturbances.

Students: Amy Huang, Liam Lloyd, Joon Lee, Viva Ojha

  • New perspectives on flexibility in simple temporal planning (2018)
  • Measuring and optimizing durability against scheduling disturbances (2019)

Multi-robot coordination

When a team of robots shares a plan, every delay ripples. We study how robots can schedule robust interactions, and how much communication and rescheduling that really takes.

Past projects

Robot Brunch & DREAM

We want multi-robot plans that adapt to events beyond the robots' control, like slippage or sensor failures. This project introduced robustness, the likelihood that a multi-robot plan succeeds, and DREAM, a single streamlined algorithm that trades robustness against computational overhead, including multi-agent communication.

Students: Jeb Brooks, Emi Reed, Alex Gruver, Kyle Lund, Sam Dietrich, Scott Chow, Liam Lloyd, Jordan Abrahams

  • Robustness in probabilistic temporal planning (2015)
  • Robust multi-robot scheduling (2016)
  • Robust execution strategies for probabilistic temporal planning (student abstract) (2016)
  • Robust execution of probabilistic temporal plans (2017)
  • Trade-offs between communication, rescheduling, and success rate in uncertain multi-agent schedules (2018)
  • Dream: An algorithm for mitigating the overhead of robust rescheduling (2019)

Joining the lab

HEATlab is not currently recruiting for any projects.

This applies to Harvey Mudd students, students at other colleges, high school students, and graduate students. If that changes, positions will be posted on Harvey Mudd's Undergraduate Research Opportunities site.

I'm a high school student. Can I do research or an internship here?

Unfortunately, no. HEATlab does not host high school students, and Jim isn't able to reply to individual requests. We'd encourage you to look for summer programs designed for high schoolers at universities near you.

I'm a graduate student or applying to PhD programs. Can I join?

Harvey Mudd is an undergraduate-only college, so there are no graduate students, PhD positions, or postdocs in the lab.

I'm a Harvey Mudd student. How can I get involved later?

Any future openings will be posted on the Undergraduate Research Opportunities site (HMC login required). In the meantime, the best preparation is coursework: CS 151 (Artificial Intelligence) and strong programming and math foundations. Harvey Mudd's CS summer research program lists projects across the whole department.

I'm a student at another college. Are there summer positions?

HEATlab has no positions right now. Harvey Mudd's CS summer research program describes opportunities across the department, including any open to students from other colleges.
